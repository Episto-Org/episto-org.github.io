Flag guide

Every kind of problem Episto flags, what it means, and what evidence a flag needs. A flag always describes a specific claim, figure, table or dataset, never a person.

Severity

CriticalThe flagged result cannot be relied on at all.
MajorThe flagged conclusion is not supported as stated. Parts of the work may still hold.
CautionThe result may hold, but should not be relied on without the stated limitation.
Builds on flagged workNot flagged itself, but cites work flagged Critical or Major. Computed by the build, never proposed.

Categories

  • Integrity: Is the data genuine and honestly reported?
  • Evidence basis: Is there a scientific test behind the claim at all?
  • Study design: Could this design answer the question?
  • Sample: Who or what was studied, and was it enough?
  • Numbers and statistics: Are the numbers and analyses right?
  • Reasoning and conclusions: Do the conclusions follow from the results?
  • Sources and citations: Does it rest on sound, correctly cited work?
  • Transparency and reproducibility: Can others check and repeat it?
  • Conflicts of interest: Who had a stake, and was it disclosed?
  • Official notices: What have the journal or publisher said?

Integrity

Is the data genuine and honestly reported?

Fabricated data

Critical

The reported data cannot have come from the study as described.

Evidence required

  • Patterns that genuine data cannot produce, shown on several values (for example repeated GRIM/SPRITE failures, or duplicated sequences or rows in shared raw data), with the calculation attached
  • Or an institutional or publisher finding, linked

Not enough on its own

  • A single inconsistent value (use stats.impossible-values)
  • Results that look "too good to be true"
  • Digit-distribution tests such as Benford's law on their own

How to check it yourself: GRIM, GRIMMER, SPRITE, Carlisle baseline test

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Falsification/Fabrication of Data; Falsification/Fabrication of Results.

Altered data or results

Major Critical

Data or results were changed, or observations removed, without disclosure, so the reported results differ from what was observed.

Evidence required

  • Side-by-side comparison of raw or registered data with what is reported
  • Or an institutional or publisher finding, linked

Not enough on its own

  • Disclosed exclusions the proposer disagrees with (use sample.exclusions)

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Manipulation of Results; Manipulation of Data.

Duplicated, manipulated or generated images

Major Critical

Figure images are duplicated, spliced, cloned, altered or generated by software (including generative models) in a way that changes what they show or presents synthetic images as observations.

Evidence required

  • Annotated image pairs or overlays with figure and panel labels
  • Tool name and settings, so anyone can reproduce the finding

Not enough on its own

  • Compression artefacts or contrast adjustment applied to the whole image

How to check it yourself: ImageJ, Forensically

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Duplication of/in Image; Manipulation of Images; Falsification/Fabrication of Image.

Same data published as new

Major

The same data or results appear in another paper, presented as new without cross-reference. Reviews and meta-analyses then count one dataset twice.

Evidence required

  • Both papers, with the matching samples, tables or results side by side

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Duplication of/in Article; Duplication of Data; Salami Slicing.

Copied data or results

Major Critical

Data or results were taken from another source and presented as the authors' own findings.

Evidence required

  • The original source and the copied material side by side

Not enough on its own

  • Copied wording without copied data or results (out of scope)

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Plagiarism of Data; Plagiarism of Image.

Paper-mill product

Critical

The paper matches the template of mass-produced fraudulent papers.

Evidence required

  • Shared images, text or data with unrelated papers, listed
  • Or a publisher investigation, linked

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Paper Mill.

Nonsense or machine-generated content

Major Critical

Text or analysis is generated or garbled ("tortured phrases") rather than describing real work.

Evidence required

  • Excerpts with each problem phrase and its standard term

Not enough on its own

  • Suspicion of AI-assisted writing when the content is coherent and correct

How to check it yourself: Problematic Paper Screener

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Computer-Aided Content or Computer-Generated Content; Hoax Paper.

False ethics, registration or data claims

Major

The paper cites an ethics approval, trial registration or data deposit that does not exist or does not match the study.

Evidence required

  • Registry or repository lookup showing the number is absent or refers to a different study
  • Or a statement from the named institution

Compromised peer review

Major

Peer review was faked or manipulated, so the paper was never independently checked.

Evidence required

  • A publisher statement, or documentary proof that reviewer identities were fake

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Compromised Peer Review; Rogue Editor.

Evidence basis

Is there a scientific test behind the claim at all?

No test of the claim

Major Critical

The claim is presented as a finding, but no data, experiment, observation or analysis in the paper tests it.

Evidence required

  • The claim, quoted
  • Where the methods and results were checked, and why none of them test it

Not enough on its own

  • Papers labelled as essays, commentaries or hypotheses that do not present their claims as findings

Claim cannot be tested

Major

The claim is stated so that no possible observation could count against it, yet it is presented as established.

Evidence required

  • The claim, quoted
  • Why no outcome would contradict it, with the paper's own wording

Anecdotes presented as general evidence

Major

Case reports, testimonials or personal experience are used to support a general claim.

Evidence required

  • The general claim, quoted
  • That its support consists of individual cases or testimonials, with counts

Not enough on its own

  • A case report that describes its case without generalising

Opinion or authority only

Caution Major

An empirical claim rests only on expert opinion, consensus or the authors' standing, with no data behind it.

Evidence required

  • The claim, quoted, and the references it relies on, shown to contain no data

Study design

Could this design answer the question?

Missing or unsuitable comparison

Major

An effect is claimed without a control or comparison group, or the comparison group differs in ways that explain the result.

Evidence required

  • The effect claim, quoted
  • The design description showing no comparison, or the relevant difference

Obvious confounder not addressed

Caution Major

A factor that plausibly drives both the exposure and the outcome was not measured, adjusted for or discussed.

Evidence required

  • The confounder, with published evidence that it affects both the exposure and the outcome
  • That the paper neither adjusts for it nor discusses it

Not enough on its own

  • A list of possible confounders without evidence that each matters

Selection on or adjustment for a common effect

Caution Major

The analysis selects the sample on, or adjusts for, a variable caused by both the exposure and the outcome (a collider), or adjusts for a consequence of the exposure, creating or hiding an association.

Evidence required

  • The variable and how the paper selects or adjusts on it
  • Why it is caused by both the exposure and the outcome (or by the exposure), with a causal diagram or published evidence

Not enough on its own

  • That a different adjustment set would also be reasonable

Immortal-time or other time-related bias

Major Critical

Follow-up time during which the outcome could not occur in one group is counted in its favour, or exposure is defined using information from after the start of follow-up.

Evidence required

  • How the paper defines the start of follow-up and the exposure
  • The period that is misclassified, and why it favours one group

Not enough on its own

  • That a target-trial design would have been better in general

No randomisation or blinding where the claim needs it

Caution Major

A causal or treatment claim is made from a design where allocation or assessment could be biased, with no randomisation or blinding.

Evidence required

  • The claim, and the allocation and assessment procedure as described

Measure does not capture the claim

Caution Major

The instrument, proxy or outcome measure does not measure what the conclusion is about, or has not been validated for this use.

Evidence required

  • The measure and the claim side by side
  • Validation literature, or its absence, for this population and use

Misidentified or contaminated materials

Major Critical

Cell lines, organisms, reagents or antibodies were not what the paper says they were.

Evidence required

  • A register entry (for example ICLAC or Cellosaurus) or authentication data

How to check it yourself: ICLAC register, Cellosaurus

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Contamination of Cell Lines/Tissues; Contamination of Materials; Error in Cell Lines/Tissues.

Instrument or measurement artefact

Major Critical

The result is produced by equipment fault, calibration error or a known artefact of the method.

Evidence required

  • The artefact demonstrated, or documented for this instrument or method

Wrong timescale or dose for the claim

Caution Major

Follow-up, exposure or dose is too short or too small for the claimed effect to appear or be ruled out.

Evidence required

  • The claim, and published evidence on the timescale or dose it needs

Unvalidated model presented as a finding

Caution Major

Results come from a model or simulation whose key assumptions were not tested, and are presented as empirical findings.

Evidence required

  • The assumptions that drive the result, and why they are untested
  • Ideally, the result changing under a reasonable alternative assumption

Sample

Who or what was studied, and was it enough?

Sample too small for the claim

Caution Major

The sample cannot reliably detect or estimate the effect claimed.

Evidence required

  • A power or precision calculation (for example confidence interval width) using the paper's own numbers

Not enough on its own

  • A claim that the sample "seems small", with no calculation

Sample does not match the claim

Caution Major

The claim covers a population that the sample does not represent (convenience, single site, WEIRD, one sex or age group).

Evidence required

  • The generalising claim, quoted, and the sample description

Selection bias

Major

Who ended up in the sample depends on the outcome or on factors linked to it (self-selection, survivorship, selecting on the outcome).

Evidence required

  • The recruitment or inclusion route, and how it links to the outcome

Dropout or missing data not handled

Caution Major

High or unequal dropout or missing data could explain the result, and the analysis does not deal with it.

Evidence required

  • Numbers enrolled and analysed per group, and the handling described

Undisclosed or after-the-fact exclusions

Major

Participants or data points were excluded without reporting, or by rules set after seeing results.

Evidence required

  • Numbers that do not add up between stages, or rules that differ from the registration

Numbers and statistics

Are the numbers and analyses right?

Impossible or inconsistent numbers

Caution Major Critical

Reported values cannot all be true: means impossible for the sample size, p-values that do not match test statistics, totals that do not add up.

Evidence required

  • Each inconsistent value, with the recalculation
  • Whether the inconsistency affects the conclusion (sets severity)

How to check it yourself: GRIM, GRIMMER, SPRITE, statcheck

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Error in Data.

Wrong test or broken assumptions

Major

The statistical method does not fit the data or design, and using the right one changes the conclusion.

Evidence required

  • Why the method does not fit
  • The result with a suitable method, or why it cannot be computed from what is reported

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Error in Analyses.

Pseudoreplication

Major

Repeated measurements of the same unit are treated as independent, inflating the sample size.

Evidence required

  • The true independent units and the n used in the analysis

Selective analysis (p-hacking)

Major

Several analyses were evidently tried and the favourable ones reported, without disclosure.

Evidence required

  • Undisclosed analytic choices shown against a registration, earlier version or the paper's own data
  • Or a clustering of key p-values just under the threshold across the paper, with the list

Not enough on its own

  • A single p-value near the threshold

How to check it yourself: p-curve

Many tests, no correction

Caution Major

Many comparisons were run and the significant few reported as findings, without correction or acknowledgement.

Evidence required

  • The number of tests run and which results survive correction

Outcome switched from registration

Major

The primary outcome or hypothesis in the paper differs from the registration, without disclosure.

Evidence required

  • Registry snapshot and paper side by side, with dates

How to check it yourself: ClinicalTrials.gov history, OSF registries, COMPare

Outcomes measured but not reported

Caution Major

Outcomes the protocol, registration or methods say were measured are missing from the results, with no explanation.

Evidence required

  • The protocol, registration or methods section listing the outcome
  • That the results and supplements do not report it

Not enough on its own

  • Outcomes the reader would have liked that were never planned

How to check it yourself: ClinicalTrials.gov history, OSF registries, COMPare

Meta-analysis counts or extracts wrongly

Caution Major Critical

A meta-analysis counts the same participants or study twice, takes effect sizes or variances from the wrong place, mixes incompatible measures, or includes studies its own criteria exclude.

Evidence required

  • The included studies concerned, with the numbers the meta-analysis used and the numbers in the source
  • The pooled estimate recomputed without the error, where possible

Not enough on its own

  • Disagreement with the inclusion criteria themselves

After-the-fact hypothesis presented as planned

Caution Major

A hypothesis formed after seeing results is presented as tested in advance.

Evidence required

  • An earlier version, preprint or registration with a different hypothesis

Circular analysis or data leakage

Major Critical

The same data select and test the effect, or test data leak into training, so the result is built in.

Evidence required

  • The step where selection and testing share data, or where leakage occurs

"No significant difference" read as "no effect"

Caution Major

A non-significant result is presented as showing that there is no effect or that groups are equal.

Evidence required

  • The claim, and the confidence interval showing meaningful effects remain possible

Misleading effect size

Caution

The effect is presented in a way that misleads about its size, such as relative risk without absolute risk.

Evidence required

  • The presented figure and the plain figure from the same data

Aggregation error

Caution Major

A group-level pattern is claimed for individuals, or a pattern reverses when the data are split or combined (ecological fallacy, Simpson's paradox).

Evidence required

  • The level of the data and the level of the claim, or the reversed pattern shown

Regression to the mean read as an effect

Major

Units selected for extreme values are re-measured, and their natural drift back is presented as an effect.

Evidence required

  • The selection on extreme values and the absence of a comparison group

Calculation or code error

Major Critical

A specific error in calculation or analysis code changes the reported results.

Evidence required

  • The error located in the authors' code or calculation, and the corrected result

Reasoning and conclusions

Do the conclusions follow from the results?

Conclusion goes beyond the results

Caution Major

The abstract or conclusion claims more than the results show.

Evidence required

  • The conclusion and the relevant result side by side

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Error in Results and/or Conclusions.

Title or abstract reports a different result

Caution Major

The title or abstract presents a secondary, subgroup or within-group finding as the main result, or leaves out a primary result that did not support the hypothesis.

Evidence required

  • The title or abstract and the primary result in the paper, side by side

Not enough on its own

  • Wording that is enthusiastic but matches the results

Overgeneralisation

Caution Major

Results are extended beyond the population, species, setting or conditions studied (for example mice to humans) without evidence.

Evidence required

  • The generalising claim and the conditions actually studied

Cause claimed from correlation

Caution Major

A causal conclusion is drawn from a design that can show only association.

Evidence required

  • The causal wording, quoted, and the design

Paper contradicts itself

Caution Major

The abstract, text, tables or figures state incompatible things.

Evidence required

  • The contradicting passages, quoted with locations

Invalid argument

Caution Major

The conclusion does not follow logically from its premises (circular reasoning, false dichotomy, affirming the consequent).

Evidence required

  • Premises and conclusion, quoted, and where the logic breaks

Error in derivation or proof

Major Critical

A mathematical or theoretical derivation contains an error that the result depends on.

Evidence required

  • The faulty step, and a counterexample or the corrected derivation

Ignores directly contrary evidence

Caution

The paper claims novelty or consensus while omitting earlier published work that directly tested the same question with a different result.

Evidence required

  • The earlier work, published before submission, on the same question

Not enough on its own

  • Work the proposer finds relevant that does not test the same question

Sources and citations

Does it rest on sound, correctly cited work?

Source does not say what is claimed

Caution Major

A cited source is used for a claim it does not make, so the error enters the literature under the source's authority.

Evidence required

  • The citing sentence, and the relevant passages of the cited source

Key claim rests on flagged work

Caution Major

A key claim depends on work that is retracted or flagged Critical or Major. Citing flagged work in passing does not qualify.

Evidence required

  • The key claim, and how it depends on the flagged work

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Cites Retracted Work.

Reference does not exist

Major

Cited works cannot be found and appear not to exist.

Evidence required

  • Each reference, with the failed lookups (DOI, catalogue, publisher)

Key claim rests on an unreliable source

Caution

A key claim relies on a withdrawn preprint, a hijacked journal or another source without genuine review, presented as established.

Evidence required

  • The claim, the source, and documentation of the source's status

How to check it yourself: Retraction Watch Hijacked Journal Checker

Irrelevant citations

Caution

Many citations are unrelated to the content, as seen in citation cartels and coercive citation.

Evidence required

  • Each irrelevant citation, and why it is unrelated to the citing text

Transparency and reproducibility

Can others check and repeat it?

Failed replication

Major Critical

Independent, adequately powered replications following the original method did not find the result.

Evidence required

  • The replication(s), linked, with their power and protocol

Not enough on its own

  • One underpowered replication, or one with a different method

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Results Not Reproducible.

Results not reproduced from the same data

Major Critical

Re-running the reported analysis on the authors' own data does not give the reported results.

Evidence required

  • The data and code used, and the differing results

Result depends on arbitrary choices

Caution Major

The result disappears under reasonable alternative analysis choices.

Evidence required

  • The alternatives run, and why each is as reasonable as the original

Data or code unavailable

Caution

Data or code the paper says are available are not, or were refused on request for a key claim.

Evidence required

  • The availability statement, and dated requests or failed links (names removed)

Also imported from retraction notices giving the reason: Original Data and/or Images not Provided and/or not Available.

Methods too vague to repeat

Caution

The methods lack details needed to repeat the study.

Evidence required

  • The missing details, listed

Conflicts of interest

Who had a stake, and was it disclosed?

A conflict of interest does not make a result wrong. It raises the risk, so these flags are Caution unless the interested party controlled the work.

Undisclosed financial interest

Caution Major

Authors had funding, employment, equity, patents or payments linked to the result and did not disclose them.

Evidence required

  • Documentary proof (funding records, patents, company filings, disclosures in other papers of the same period)
  • The paper's own disclosure statement

Undisclosed non-financial interest

Caution

Authors evaluated their own product or method, or held a documented formal role tied to the outcome, and did not disclose it.

Evidence required

  • Documentary proof of the role or ownership, and the disclosure statement

Not enough on its own

  • Inferred beliefs, politics or personal views

Funder controlled the study

Major

A party with a stake in the result controlled the design, analysis or decision to publish.

Evidence required

  • A contract, protocol, correspondence or official statement showing the control

Official notices

What have the journal or publisher said?

Retracted

Caution Critical

The paper has been retracted. A retraction whose only reasons do not concern the validity of the work (retract-and-replace, a publisher's error, withdrawal to publish elsewhere) is Caution.

Source

  • The retraction notice

Expression of concern

Major

The publisher has formally questioned the paper's reliability.

Source

  • The notice

Correction affecting conclusions

Caution Major

A published correction changes results or conclusions.

Source

  • The correction
  • and what it changes

Withdrawn preprint

Major

The authors or server withdrew the preprint.

Source

  • The withdrawal notice

Deliberately not flagged

  • Motives, politics or ideology of authors. Only their visible effects on the work are flaggable.
  • Disagreeing with consensus, novelty or unpopular conclusions. Contradicting accepted views is not an error.
  • People. Flags attach to papers and claims, never to authors, labs or institutions.
  • Writing quality, typos and copied wording without copied data or results.
  • Ethics violations that do not affect validity. These belong with the institution or journal.
  • Journal prestige or impact factor.

Episto flags specific problems with specific research, decided by randomly drawn panels of pseudonymous reviewers. Flags are not judgements of people.
